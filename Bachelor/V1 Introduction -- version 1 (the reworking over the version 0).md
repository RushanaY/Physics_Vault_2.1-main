### 1.1 historic usage of hydrogen to combine theory and reality 
The hydrogen atom spans the bridge between the theoretical models and experimental reality of Quantum electrodynamics and generally Quantum physics. 
Since the observation of the discrete lines of the Balmer series in the atomic hydrogen spectrum by Anders Angstrom in 1853, the hydrogen atom became a key player in the science of small particles. Later this series was formulated into the Rydberg equation by Johannes Rydberg in 1888, that was the generalisation of the Balmer equation and now applicable for the spectrum for any other particles. This equation describes the wavelength of the discrete line, which correlates with the difference between two neighbouring energy levels of a particle.  $$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H (\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n^2_2})$$

While both the experimental data and the equation connect very well, this mathematical description was purely empirical and did not find a good explanation in the classical physics of that time period. This changed with the development of the quantum theory in the upcoming 20th century. It started with the atomic model of Niels Bohr, which despite being debated on its accuracy, is still a very good description of the atom and explanation of the observed hydrogen spectra of the last century. The idea is that an electron is orbiting a positively charged nucleus on discrete orbits. The electron can jump up and down those orbits , which are called the discrete energy levels. In Erwin Schroedingers work these got assigned the quantum number $n$, in addition to the angular momentum quantum numbers $l$ and $m$. In his work he described these energy levels, so that the simplest energy structure of a hydrogen atom got this mathematical equation: $$E_n = -hc \frac{R_{\infty}}{n^2}$$


The expression from above still didn't have the relativistic component and experiments demonstrated, that the hydrogen structure is still not fully understood. This step to brining in the quantum mechanics into this equation was done by Dirac in 1928. He was able to describe the concept of the fine structure, that was reason for the observation of energy level splitting. The basis for that was set years earlier in 1921 in the Stern Gerlach experiment, where it was shown that electrons posses a spin, which is the reason for the splitting of the energy levels. Another discrepancy between the theoretical Dirac equation for the energy levels and the real experiment was explained in the 1940x. It was the Lamb shift, that stemmed from the way the electron is impacted by vacuum energy fluctuations. This became the first step to develop the science of Quantum electrodynamics. Calculating the Lamb shift and explaining the discrepancy in the hydrogen atom open the gateway for the actually application of the QED theories.

The reason why the hydrogen atom plays such a crucial part in the QED is because of it simple structure. It is simple enough so that the transition frequencies between the two energy levels can be calculated theoretically and compared with real life experiments. The hydrogen atom is made up from one electron, which orbits one proton and they both a bound together by Coulomb force. The energy levels, that the electron can inhabit are described in modern QED by the following equation $$E_{n,l,j} = h c \textbf{R_{\infty}} (- \frac{1}{b^2} + f_{n,l,j} (\alpha, \frac{m_e}{m_p}, ...) + \delta_{l,0}\frac{C_{N,S}{n^3} \textbf{r_p^2}$$
where n,l,j are the principle, angular and total angular momentum quantum numbers, $f_{n,l,j}$ is the QED series in the fine structure constant $\alpha$ and the last part is the correction factor.  
The most precise transition to measure the factors of the Rydberg constant $R_{\infty}$ and the proton radius $r_p$ is the 1S-2S transition. The reason is the rather long lifetime of the 2S state. Another very accurate measurement was done in 2013 on muonic hydrogen. Due to the higher mass mass of the muon, the orbit around the nucleus is smaller, which makes the electron much more sensitive to the finite size of the proton. These values were considered to be the most precise values for the proton radius and all the other data were compared to them. This started the "proton radius puzzle" in the CODATA 2014 paper, in which the 1S-2S value was compared to the muonic one and they were found to be more than 1.9 $\sigma$ apart from eachother. The later measurements were there to solve this puzzle. In the resent paper in 2026 the proton puzzle was solved by using the 2S-6P transition. Their value corresponds with the muonic hydrogen value, hence solving the problem 

In that time and based on the previous works of the scientist Niels Bohr proposed the Bohr's orbital model of an atom. In this model a heavy , positively charged nucleus is orbited by the light, negatively charged  electron, which stays on its designated, discrete orbits. The system is held together by Coloumb force. This model was used by Erwin Schrodinger in the year 1936 in order to describe the first equation for the energy levels, using not only the principle quantum number $n$, but also the angular momentum numbers $l$ and $m$. 
In the upcoming years the science of Quantum physics rapidly developed, leading the way into the quantum effects, that happen in inside even the simplest atom, like the hydrogen. In 1928 the work of Dirac set up the equation for the splitting of the energy levels, which is called the fine structure. After that in 1947 the Lamb shift, that stems form vacuum fluctiations in the electromagnetic field , was found. In the later develepement of the quantum electrodnamics came another splittin - the hyperfne structure. 
Especially with the discovery of the spin of an electron, that was shown during the Stern Gerlach experiment, it became clear that the energy levels need a correction factor. This meant that the levels before weren't described fully and are missing the factor, that comes from the spin of the electron interacting in the system. Described as the fine structure, it defined the levels closer. ## 1.2 The proton puzzle 
As seen above, the energy levels heavily depend on the correction factor, that stems form the volume effect. Meaning that the nucleus, the proton in this case, have a certain size, which cannot the overlooked. The initial term for the energy levels works only of the nucleus is assumed to be a point charge, which of course is not the case in the real world. This factor, while being small, is significant enough to be seen during highly precise spectroscopy of hydrogen. Especially hydrogen has more impacted by this effect. On one side its nucleus, the proton is very well understood, due to its rather simple structure of three quarks. On the other the atom is more affected by quantum fluctuations, as compared to heavier nuclei, where these effects can be averaged out. 
It is possible to overcome the difficulties, by using muonic hydrogen. Here one electron is swapped for a muon, which decreases the Bohr radius due to the muon being 207 times heavier than the electron. This makes the muonic hydrogen much more impacted by the finite proton radius, leading to a very precise value. This value can be tested by a measurement on normal hydrogen, which was the goal of the ....

*Using the transition frequency scientist can determine the size of the nucleus of a hydrogen. The nucleus in this case being the proton. Over the last decades the proton radius was measured by a multitude of scientific groups and up to date the most presice one is the value that was gotten from the muonic hydrogen. Because of its heavier, the electrons orbit the proton at a closer distance. This negaties some of the quantum effects and also is not dependand on the Rydberg constant. Other measurements done on normal hyrogen showed not only much larger error bars, but the value also did not much, even inside the error bar interval. The groups measuring the proton on normal hydrogen after, were able to come cloer to the muonic level with each step, until the proton puzzle was solved this year, but a group in the MPQ. Their value coinsides with the muonic one to a never seen before presision, proving that the muonic measurement also gave the correct proton radius value. # 2. 1S-3S experiment itself  
The 1S-3S group is working with the transition 1s to 3S . Using a TiSa laser they excite the hydrogen atoms inside the hydrogen beam. The atomic hydrogen is created by a plasma, that dissacociates the molecular hydrogen. 
In order to have reliable data, it is important to have at least some and at best consistent amout of atomic hydrogen in the flux. For that purpuse there are multiple ways to do that. One is to use a pressure gauge(?), which shows that there is a difference depending on the ratio of atomic to molecular particles, but due to the working process of a gauge, this isn't a very reliable method, especially considering that plasma (ionized stuff) is used in the setup. ANother way is to take the spectrum of the plasma itself and dedcue form there how high the disaccosiation ratio is, by analysing the Fulcher alpha line. This is a very presice method, but the actual spectroscopy is being done further away form the plasma, meaning that the atmoic hydrogen will recombine on the way there. A very promising approach was to scan the beam as it is leaving the nozzle, right before it is being analyzed. This can be done with the wire detectro, which is the main topic of this thesis.
